My first marathon was Utrecht, May 2023: 4:18, no coach and plenty of mistakes. Three and a half years and seven marathons later, I ran 2:56:47 in Amsterdam. In between came Rotterdam, Valencia twice, Rome, Lake Garda and Berlin: personal bests, bad days, and one sub-3 missed by 11 seconds. Here are 15 lessons from the whole journey, whether you're training for your first marathon or chasing your first sub-3.

## Every workout matters, even if it doesn’t look good. Trust the process

Some sessions will feel heavy, slow and ugly, and your watch will judge you. Ignore it. Fitness is the sum of many workouts, not a highlight reel of perfect ones. A bad interval session on tired legs still teaches your body to work under fatigue, which is exactly what kilometer 35 feels like. Don't judge your form by a single day; look at the trend over weeks. Show up, do the work, let the plan do its job.

## Heart rate is key: during workouts and during the race

Pace lies, heart rate doesn't. Wind, hills, heat and a bad night's sleep all change what a given pace costs you, and heart rate hands you the bill honestly. In training it keeps easy days easy and tells you when you are not recovered. In the race it is your early-warning system: if it drifts up in the first half, you are borrowing energy from kilometer 30, and the interest rate is brutal. Learn what marathon heart rate feels like in training, then trust it over the adrenaline on race day.

One tip: buy a chest strap heart rate monitor. Other types are unreliable, and the optical sensor on your watch is almost useless while running. Cadence lock, a loose strap or a cold wrist, and it shows numbers that have nothing to do with your heart. If you are going to trust a number, make sure it is the right one.

And if you're serious about training by heart rate, functional diagnostics is a good investment. A lab test determines your precise heart rate zones and your current VO2 max, instead of relying on formulas or your watch's estimates.

## Carbs are your friend. So is water

Marathon pace at sub-3 runs on carbohydrates, and your tank is small. This is not the time for your keto experiment. Eat enough carbs during heavy weeks, not only before the race, and practice fueling in long runs so your stomach learns to handle gels at race pace instead of filing a complaint at kilometer 25. In the race, take carbs and water early and regularly, long before you feel you need them. By the time you are hungry, thirsty or dizzy, it is too late. Grabbing a cup at race pace without wearing it is a skill too, so practice that as well.

## Strength training is critical, and not just for legs

Strength work is not about bigger legs. In the last kilometers it is not your legs that collapse first, it's your posture, and that is held up by your core, hips and glutes. A strong core keeps you upright and efficient; strong hips keep your knees out of trouble. Fewer injuries means more weeks of uninterrupted training, and consistency beats everything. Two short sessions a week are enough, as long as you don't "forget" them.

## Mobility work is critical. 50% is running, 50% is mobility work

Yes, 50%. Every kilometer makes you a bit tighter, and tight muscles change your stride until something breaks. Mobility work after runs is what lets your body absorb the volume a sub-3 plan asks for. It doesn't have to be long, but it has to be as regular as the runs. The runner who stays healthy for the whole block beats the one who trained harder for half of it and then watched the race on TV.

## Painkillers are a bad idea for running

Pain is information, and painkillers just mute the messenger. If you need a pill to run, something is wrong, and running through it turns a two-week niggle into a two-month break. In a marathon it's worse: anti-inflammatories plus dehydration plus three hours of effort is a real risk for your kidneys. Fix the cause instead: rest, see a physio, adjust the plan.

## Champion in training or champion in the race. No ego lifting

You can win every workout or you can win the race, rarely both. Smashing intervals faster than planned feels great, and it steals energy from the next session and from race day. Racing your long runs for Strava kudos is ego, not training. Nobody will remember your fastest Tuesday. Hit the target paces, not faster, and save the hero moments for the race, where they actually count.

## The taper will feel slower. It should. Everything is fine

During the taper your legs feel heavy, flat and suspiciously slow, and your head starts to doubt everything. Welcome, this is normal. Your body is repairing and refueling, and that doesn't feel like fitness. You won't lose your form in two or three easier weeks, but you can absolutely ruin your race with a panic workout "just to check". Trust the plan. The freshness shows up on race day, not before.

And most importantly, do nothing, and I mean NOTHING, on the Saturday before the race. April 2025, Italy, Lake Garda. My first sub-3 attempt, and I felt great and ready. But I only had 4 days there, and I was flying back to NL right after the race. So I decided to go for a 10 km hike with 500 m of elevation gain. Was it stupid? Hell yes, it was! During the race, however, it didn't show until the last 5 kilometers, when my legs just gave up.

Most runners stay in the city until Monday to do some sightseeing, and that is a smart idea. It's just not for me: I leave the crime scene right away. I win, and I leave.

## All out till the finish line. Leave no room for tomorrow’s regrets

Valencia, December 2025. I felt super ready, with a high chance of sub-3 after a successful progression run. Carb-loaded, well rested, amazing execution. I was looking only at my average pace, which was around 4:15, and thought I was perfectly on time, even slightly ahead. Small detail: my watch measured the distance I ran, not the 42.195 km the timing mat cares about. So I started to fuck around: engaging with the crowd, taking out my phone to take some photos, etc. My official time missed sub-3 by 11 seconds. To say those 11 seconds haunted me in my nightmares is to say nothing. It was a stupid mistake, but it was mine to learn from, so here it is.

A sub-3 often comes down to seconds, and those seconds live in the last kilometers, where it hurts most and where it is easiest to give up a little. Don't. Empty the tank before the line, not after it. Tomorrow you can rest; today you finish what you trained for. Selfies are for after the medal.

## Find your working mantra for the hard workouts and last kilometers

When the body hurts, the mind starts negotiating, and it is a very good negotiator. A short mantra gives your head something to hold on to instead. Keep it positive and simple; "please let this end" doesn't count. Test it in hard workouts, so it already works on race day.

During my last progression run, I wanted to stop after kilometer 4 of 28. The phrase I kept repeating was "You already did what you thought was impossible", and it kept me going. Later, when I started the last leg at lactate threshold, it was really painful. I repeated something Mark Dowdle, an American ultrarunner, said around the 70th lap of the Last Man Standing backyard ultra: "Every step is evidence. The story in your head is not true." Use these, or find what pushes you through a tough challenge.

## It’s your race. Pacers and friends won’t help you. You’re on your own

Pacers, groups and friends on the course are nice, but they can't run a single kilometer for you. Know your own plan, your own pace and your own heart rate, and run it no matter who is around you.

At the 2026 Amsterdam Marathon, I started at a 4:25 pace and let the 3:00 pacers go immediately. Around kilometer 30, I overtook the first two of them. A pace group looks like a free ride, but in reality it is a tight crowd of nervous people, where one clipped heel at a water station ends your day. No, thank you.

There is nothing positive about a positive split. Start slower than your target pace and let your body wake up and fully switch on. The time you "lose" in the first kilometers is nothing compared to what you lose by blowing up after 30.

## Zone 2 training is overrated

Easy running matters. Religiously staying in Zone 2 doesn't. For many runners it means shuffling so slowly that it doesn't even resemble their own stride, and then wondering why race pace feels foreign. A sub-3 needs time at marathon pace, threshold work and long runs with quality in them. Easy days should be easy enough to recover, not locked to a number some podcast told you about. Use Zone 2 as a tool, not a religion.

## Plated shoes work, but train in simple shoes

Carbon plates work; use them on race day. For daily training, normal shoes make your feet, ankles and calves do the work, and that makes you stronger. Running everything in super shoes hides your weaknesses and burns through very expensive foam. Save the plated pair for key workouts, a few long runs and the race, and test them over a long distance first. The shoe helps, but the engine is still you.

## Work with a coach

Like everyone, I started running without a coach and did several half marathons and one marathon on my own. Needless to say, I made so many mistakes, even after reading and researching the topic. I've now been working with my coach for the last 3 years, with almost no injuries, steady progress and, most importantly, results.

A coach sees what you can't see in yourself. Left alone, you go too hard when you feel good and skip things when you don't. A coach builds the plan around your life and your weak spots, changes it when things go wrong, and has seen dozens of athletes make the exact mistake you are about to make. Best of all, they take the decisions off your shoulders: you just execute. Someone checking your log every week also does wonders for motivation on rainy Tuesdays. If sub-3 is the goal, this is the best money you will spend. Yes, better spent than on the shoes.

## Celebration time has to be planned in

The race is the goal, but it is not the end of the story. After months of discipline, the celebration deserves a plan too: book the dinner, invite the people who supported you, and take real time off running.

After the Amsterdam Marathon, I hadn't planned a celebration and went hiking in Switzerland with my son right away. Don't get me wrong: my little one is my greatest supporter, and I'm his hero, so he was super happy for my achievement. The trip wasn't the problem. The hurry was.

I wanted to leave early on Sunday afternoon to beat the traffic and cover as many kilometers as possible that day. Within a few hours of the finish line, I was packing the car instead of letting it sink in. That rush took away a big part of the joy of an incredible achievement: the sub-3 marathon I had dreamed about for so long. Staying for a beer with friends from the club would have made it so much nicer. Instead, I celebrated my dream sub-3 on the motorway.

Celebrate the work, not only the time on the clock.
